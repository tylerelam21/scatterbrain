# Claude Brief — Outward-Facing Portfolio Homepage

I want to evolve the current visual language of my site into an **outward-facing personal portfolio**.

The current page has a design language I really like: warm ivory background, large editorial serif typography, muted forest green, small handwritten elements, lots of breathing room, and an understated almost literary quality.

However, the current implementation reads too much like a **personal productivity dashboard** because of elements like Today, Calendar, weather, sticky notes, events, Settings, Sign Out, etc.

For the public-facing portfolio, I want to preserve the underlying aesthetic while changing the experience completely.

The goal should be for someone visiting the site to feel like they have entered **Tyler’s little world** rather than opened Tyler’s productivity software.

The site should feel somewhere between:

**an illustrated studio, a field journal, a cabinet of curiosities, and a beautifully designed editorial portfolio.**

It should still feel sophisticated and intentional. I do not want it to become a scrapbook or novelty website.

---

## Overall Concept

The outward-facing homepage should communicate three things very quickly:

**I make things.**
**I have a point of view.**
**There is an actual person behind the work.**

I want the personality of the site to come from physical objects, illustrations, typography, small annotations, and storytelling rather than excessive interface chrome.

The central idea is:

> **Don’t make my portfolio look like a portfolio. Make it feel like a place that belongs to me.**

The more expressive parts of the site should invite exploration, while the actual project presentation underneath should remain clean and professional.

Think:

**Illustration and personality at the top.
Strong portfolio design underneath.**

---

# 1. Simplify the Header

The current header feels like a web application.

Remove public-facing items such as:

`Search`
`Today`
`Calendar`
`Settings`
`Sign out`

Instead, use a very minimal portfolio navigation.

Something along the lines of:

**[Rune] Tyler Elam**

with navigation on the opposite side:

**Work · About · Notes · Contact**

The exact language can evolve, but the important thing is that it feels like a personal studio or portfolio, not SaaS software.

Keep the rune in the upper-left. It should function as the personal mark for the site.

The header should remain thin, restrained, and almost disappear into the page.

---

# 2. Keep the Editorial Hero

I really like the enormous serif treatment currently being used for the date.

Repurpose that same visual confidence for my identity.

For example:

# Tyler Elam

Then beneath it, a short sentence that establishes what I do without sounding like generic portfolio copy.

Possible direction:

**I make useful things and try to make them beautiful.**

Or something similarly understated.

I would rather this sound human than like:

> Product designer passionate about creating meaningful experiences.

Avoid standard portfolio language.

Underneath the main statement, retain one small handwritten piece of personality.

The current quote:

**“There’s some good in this world, and it’s worth fighting for.”**

could remain somewhere in the experience because I like what it contributes emotionally.

Likewise, something subtle like:

*Still paying attention.*

could work as a recurring handwritten motif.

The handwriting should feel like marginalia rather than another primary typeface.

---

# 3. Create an Illustrated “Things on My Desk” / Studio Area

This is the part of the homepage that should make the portfolio memorable.

Instead of immediately showing a grid of project cards, create a large illustrated composition occupying a substantial portion of the viewport.

It should use the same hand-drawn illustration language we have been developing elsewhere on the site.

It does **not** need to literally look like a realistic desk.

It can feel more like objects arranged on a page, studio wall, pegboard, field table, or collected workspace.

Possible objects include:

* the hand-drawn **pegboard with painter and workshop tools**
* the **Tolkien-inspired map** representing Alpharetta → Athens → Atlanta / my story
* a **notebook or sketchbook**
* the **rune / green round-door motif**
* a few **photographs or contact-sheet images**
* **paint swatches, brushes, tape, pencils, or material samples**
* a pinned note, scrap of paper, small map fragment, ticket, or other subtle artifacts

These objects should function as navigation.

For example, the pegboard could lead into professional work, the map could lead into my story/About page, the notebook could lead to writing or notes, photography could open a visual archive, and the rune/door could become a playful entrance into the personal side of the site.

The exact mapping can evolve.

What matters is that the navigation feels **discovered rather than presented as six rectangular cards**.

Small handwritten labels can appear near objects or on hover.

Animations should be restrained: tiny lifts, paper movement, pencil marks, subtle rotation, shadows, or annotations appearing.

Nothing should bounce around like a game UI.

---

# 4. Make the Illustration Feel Physical

I want more texture and materiality than the current dashboard has.

The website can include visual elements inspired by:

ink drawings, pencil marks, slightly crooked paper, masking tape, folded maps, notebook pages, stamps, paint chips, little arrows, imperfect borders, marginal notes, and faint paper shadows.

However, the underlying layout must remain extremely disciplined.

That contrast is important.

The goal is not:

**messy scrapbook.**

The goal is:

**beautiful editorial design with evidence that a human touched it.**

The imperfections should be intentional.

---

# 5. Be Restrained With Handwriting

The handwritten font is useful, but I do not want it everywhere.

Use typography hierarchically.

The **serif** should be the main visual voice.

A clean **sans serif** should handle navigation, metadata, dates, small labels, and utility text.

The **handwriting** should be reserved for comments, little annotations, captions, arrows, notes, and occasional personality.

Think of handwriting as something I wrote in the margin after the page had already been designed.

That will make it much more effective.

---

# 6. Transition Into a Very Clean “Selected Work” Section

After the illustrated studio/object section, the page should become noticeably calmer.

This is where the visitor sees the actual work.

Use a large editorial heading:

# Selected Work

Avoid a generic four-column portfolio card grid.

Projects should feel more like spreads in an art/design book.

For example:

**01 — Terminus Operations**
*Designing a better way to run a painting company.*

Large project image.

Lots of white space.

Then:

**02 — Estimator**
*A field-first estimating tool built around the way contractors actually work.*

And so on.

Images should be large enough to become part of the visual composition rather than thumbnails.

Project numbers, titles, and descriptions can alternate alignment occasionally so the page has rhythm.

This section should prove that underneath the personality and illustration, there is serious work.

The hierarchy I want is:

**expressive introduction → exploration → professional proof.**

---

# 7. Avoid Conventional Portfolio “Card Chrome”

Please minimize:

rounded white cards, large drop shadows, pill badges everywhere, gradients, glassmorphism, excessive border boxes, icon-heavy interfaces, and standard startup-dashboard patterns.

The current design succeeds partly because there is so much open page around everything.

Preserve that.

Let typography, imagery, composition, and physical illustration create hierarchy rather than containers.

---

# 8. Preserve the Existing Color Language

I really like the current palette.

Keep the warm off-white / ivory background as the primary canvas.

Keep the dark muted forest green as the signature accent.

Use near-black rather than harsh pure black for most typography.

Supporting colors can include:

muted yellow, dusty/slate blue, graphite gray, warm brown, and possibly a very restrained muted brick red.

These should feel like colors coming from old maps, paint chips, paper, field notebooks, and workshop materials rather than a software design system.

Do not suddenly introduce bright product-design colors.

---

# 9. Make the Footer Personal

I do not want a generic corporate footer.

Something closer to:

**Currently in Atlanta, Georgia.**

Then a small personal sentence.

For example:

*Painting houses, building software, making things, and watching entirely too much fútbol.*

Then simple links:

**Email · LinkedIn · GitHub**

Potentially include the rune again.

The footer can feel like the final handwritten note at the bottom of a letter.

---

# 10. The Homepage Should Have Three Distinct Layers

Conceptually, the public homepage should be built around:

### Identity

A quiet editorial introduction with my name, mark, and a small amount of copy.

### World

The illustrated object/studio section where visitors can explore different parts of who I am and what I make.

### Proof

A clean, sophisticated Selected Work section with real projects and case studies.

This is probably the most important structural principle for the redesign.

---

# General Visual Direction

The site should feel:

**literary but not pretentious**
**playful but not childish**
**handmade but not sloppy**
**professional but not corporate**
**personal without oversharing**
**nostalgic without feeling retro-themed**

There are several visual influences already emerging naturally across the portfolio: the Tolkien-style map, the rune, the illustrated painting/workshop tools, paper textures, hand drawing, muted colors, and the editorial typography of the current homepage.

Those should feel like pieces of the **same visual universe**.

Do not add random whimsical objects merely because they look interesting.

Everything should feel like something that plausibly belongs to me.

---

# Important distinction from the current site

The existing screenshot can continue to serve as inspiration for the **design system**:

* typography
* spacing
* color
* restraint
* warmth
* editorial quality
* handwriting
* paper-like background

But do **not** preserve its dashboard information architecture for the outward-facing site.

The public site should not greet visitors with my calendar, weather, reminders, or productivity tools.

Instead, it should turn that same aesthetic outward:

**Who is Tyler?
What does he make?
What does he care about?
What has he actually built?**

The outward-facing site should answer those questions while still leaving enough mystery that visitors want to explore.
